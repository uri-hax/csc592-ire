### What will you be doing?

You will plan, develop, and write three hypothetical abstracts, three supporting research questions, iterate on them, and then select a final idea (your most preferred) to develop your proposal for the class. This will all be for a matter speaking `hypothetical`. But will eventually turn into your topic for the class. Pick a topic related to your research or interests. This could be deep learning, social networks, Wikipedia/WikiData, Digital Humanities, LLMs, recommendation systems, etc.

> This is a common practice in developing interesting, novel, and feasible research ideas! :)

:::note[Be Smart by being Concise]
The best writers say a lot by writing little. Be concise and clear! Have a friend who is not in CS read your writing. If they can understand what you are saying, then myself and your classmates will too! :)
:::

:::danger[Do not use previous papers]
Do you have a paper already published? If yes, you cannot directly reuse that.
Are you currently working on a research system, paper, or thesis? It is okay and encouraged to incorporate that into your ideas, or even have it be your idea! :)
:::

### Need some research ideas?

We will brainstorm ideas together in class. If you need a jump start, here are some topics and directions in Information Retrieval and Information Extraction that might help get you thinking:

**Social Media Information Extraction:** extracting useful information from noisy, informal, and rapidly changing user-generated content. Projects might examine entity extraction, event detection, stance, topic identification, or transforming social media posts into structured datasets.

**Search and Ranking:** investigating how documents, posts, or other information should be ranked in response to a query. Projects might compare retrieval models, ranking strategies, embeddings, or different ways of representing queries and documents.

**Human-Centered Information Retrieval:** studying how people search for, evaluate, and interact with information. Projects could examine search behavior, query reformulation, result presentation, user interaction logs, or how interface design affects retrieval effectiveness.

**Information Extraction Pipelines:** developing or evaluating pipelines that transform unstructured text into structured information. This could include named entity recognition, relation extraction, classification, summarization, knowledge graph construction, or extracting structured data from noisy documents.

**User Modeling and Personalization:** using interaction data, preferences, or past behavior to model users and adapt retrieval systems. Projects might explore personalized ranking, recommendations, search histories, or the tradeoffs between personalization and privacy.

**Dataset and Benchmark Construction:** examining how datasets used to evaluate retrieval and extraction systems are created. Projects could build a small benchmark, analyze existing labels, derive relevance judgments from user interactions, or investigate bias and data quality.

**Retrieval-Augmented Systems:** investigating systems that retrieve external information before generating or presenting a response. Projects might evaluate retrieval quality, compare retrieval strategies, examine citation or provenance, or study how retrieval errors affect downstream outputs.

**Knowledge Graphs and Structured Knowledge:** extracting entities and relationships from text and representing them in structured resources such as Wikidata or custom knowledge graphs. Projects might examine extraction accuracy, entity linking, missing information, or data provenance.

**Recommendation Systems:** are commonly seen in online platforms like streaming services and e-commerce websites. They use algorithms to predict and present content or products that a user is likely to appreciate, based on their past interactions and preferences.

You do not necessarily need to invent an entirely new retrieval or extraction method. A strong project can reproduce an existing approach, apply it to a new dataset or domain, compare multiple methods, extend an existing system, or critically evaluate an existing dataset, benchmark, or pipeline.

Need help, here is some handy dandy info from University of Wisconsin Madison on [Writing an Abstract for Your Research Paper](https://writing.wisc.edu/handbook/assignments/writing-an-abstract-for-your-research-paper/).

Think of these steps as guidelines translating to 1-2 sentences per step.
1. The context or background information for your research; the general topic under study; the specific topic of your research
2. The central questions or statement of the problem your research addresses
3. What’s already known about this question, what previous research has done or shown
4. The main reason(s), the exigency, the rationale, the goals for your research—Why is it important to address these questions? Are you, for example, examining a new topic? Why is that topic worth examining? Are you filling a gap in previous research? Applying new methods to take a fresh look at existing ideas or data? Resolving a dispute within the literature in your field? . . .
5. Your research and/or analytical methods
6. Your main findings, results, or arguments
7. The significance or implications of your findings or arguments.

### Example Abstract from a Short-Paper

Below is an abstract that loosely follows the above steps from the paper [Accelerating Adult Readers with
Typeface: A Study of Individual
Preferences and Effectiveness](https://shaunwallace.org/files/Accelerating_Adult_Readers_with_Typeface_A_Study_of_Individual_Preferences_and_Effectiveness.pdf).

Information overload is the challenge of the modern era and text the medium. Every adult reader would benefit from faster reading, provided they could retain comprehension. The present work explores the reading speed gains possible solely by manipulating typeface. We consider that optimal typeface might be a matter of an individual’s preferred font, or that some fonts might be better for all users. Indeed, eight in ten of our participants believed their favorite font would be their best. Instead, our findings showed that the preferred font was seldom best, and one font did not fit all. Adult readers in our study read better with varying fonts. An average 117 word per minute difference between worst and best typeface, or around 10 additional pages an hour, means font choice is of real-world significance. Our discussion focuses on the challenges of rapidly identifying an individual’s optimal font, and the exciting individuation technologies such an advance allows.

> Below are some guidelines derived from each part of the abstract!

##### Give a sentence describing the societal problem
Information overload is the challenge of the modern era and text the medium.

##### What could we research to solve this problem
Every adult reader would benefit from faster reading, provided they could retain comprehension.

##### What were our study methods
The present work explores the reading speed gains possible solely by manipulating typeface. We consider that optimal typeface might be a matter of an individual’s preferred font, or that some fonts might be better for all users. 

##### Discuss our results and possibly their potential impact
Indeed, eight in ten of our participants believed their favorite font would be their best. Instead, our findings showed that the preferred font was seldom best, and one font did not fit all. Adult readers in our study read better with varying fonts. An average 117 word per minute difference between worst and best typeface, or around 10 additional pages an hour, means font choice is of real-world significance. 

#### Discuss the future implications of our results
Our discussion focuses on the challenges of rapidly identifying an individual’s optimal font, and the exciting individuation technologies such an advance allows.
